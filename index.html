After the Fire, a Family’s Ordinary Life in Newtown

Mark Parkson looks back on the photographs, familiar rooms and half-remembered belongings of a childhood spent in a rebuilt house.

By Nora Bennett   |   September 18, 2025   |   3 min read

NEWTOWN, Conn. — Mark Parkson remembers the small things first: the patterned wallpaper in his bedroom, the sound of footsteps on the stairs, and the lamp that stayed lit in the hallway after bedtime. The fire is different. It belongs to a story he was told, not one he witnessed.

In 1972, a house on the outskirts of Newtown was severely damaged by fire. Work to rebuild the property began the following year and was completed in 1974. By the early 1980s, the Parkson family was making a life there, in rooms that looked nothing like those photographed before the blaze.

“People ask me what it was like to live in a house that had burned down. But that wasn’t how I thought about it. To me, it was just our house.”

Parkson, who has spent recent months sorting through boxes of family photographs and papers, says the difference between the building’s history and his own memories has become clearer with age.

“My parents knew the story of the place much better than I did,” he said. “They knew what had been repaired, what had been taken away. When you’re a kid, you don’t think about any of that. You care about where you keep your toys.”

A Childhood in Photographs

Among the items Parkson has kept are photographs taken in the living room, the yard and his bedroom. Some show birthdays or family visits; others appear to have been taken for no particular occasion. The dates on the backs are not always legible.

“There’s one picture where I can tell you exactly where everybody was sitting,” he said. “And another one from around the same time where I can’t remember who was holding the camera. It’s funny what stays with you.”

One bedroom photograph shows Parkson sitting on a bed beside a collection of toys. Asked about an old-fashioned doll that appears in the picture, he paused.

“I remember seeing that doll in photographs,” he said. “I don’t think I played with it very much. I couldn’t tell you where it came from, to be honest.”

He laughed at his own uncertainty. “It’s probably not much of a story. Most of the things we had back then have disappeared one way or another.”

What Survives

Property records from the period describe a reconstruction carried out in 1973 and 1974. Some elements of the earlier structure, including portions of the foundation, were retained.

Parkson says those details meant little to him until he began putting the documents alongside the family photographs.

“I used to think that keeping the pictures meant keeping the memories,” he said. “But sometimes a photograph is the only reason you remember something at all.”

For now, he plans to continue sorting the collection, labeling what he can and setting aside what he cannot identify. He hopes the photographs will eventually become a more complete record for younger relatives.

“It was a perfectly ordinary childhood in a perfectly ordinary home,” Parkson said. “That’s the part I’d like people to remember.”
