The Records We Keep: Why Old House Files Still Matter

In Newtown and neighboring communities, the history of a building is often scattered among permits, photographs and handwritten notes.

By Evelyn Carter   |   October 2026   |   5 min read

Some of western Connecticut’s most useful historical documents were never meant to be read as history. They were created to authorize a repair, record an inspection or verify that a property had changed hands. Decades later, those routine pages may be the only surviving account of what once stood on a particular lot.

In town offices and small historical collections, residents continue to help organize these materials. A parcel number might connect a building to a series of permits; a photograph might reveal that a porch was replaced or a roofline changed long before anyone thought to write the story down.

The value of these records is also their limitation. Forms are sometimes amended, copied by hand or separated from the drawings that originally accompanied them. Archivists usually compare several sources before drawing conclusions about a site.

A group of recently catalogued files concerning a Newtown-area residence illustrates the problem. The home was damaged in a fire in 1972, and reconstruction work appears in property paperwork dated 1973 and 1974.

The records show that portions of the original foundation were retained during rebuilding. Nothing in that detail is especially unusual: preserving sound masonry can be a practical choice when a structure is restored after a fire.

One inspection copy, however, lists a lower-level access point that is absent from the subsequent plan. It may simply reflect an outdated drawing. The available paperwork does not explain the difference.

A second copy reproduces the same annotation twice. The ink is darker on the second occurrence, as though someone returned to the line after completing the form.

The inspector’s final entry reads: FOUNDATION RETAINED. FOUNDATION RETAINED. FOUNDATION RETAINED.

The date of final approval is written as April 19, 1974. Beneath it appears another date, identical except for the year. The year has been crossed out so heavily that the paper is torn.

FILE 73-041 / BASEMENT ACCESS / INSPECTION INCOMPLETE / ACCESS NOT REQUIRED / ACCESS NOT PERMITTED

The door was not part of the reconstruction. The door was there before the fire. The door was there before the house.

DO NOT DIG BELOW THE FOUNDATION. DO NOT DIG BELOW THE FOUNDATION. THERE IS NO FOUNDATION.
